Why call the shot?

With public data, it's easy to fool yourself

The temptation

Anyone can download these files, try 50 ways of slicing them, and report the one that looks exciting. Researchers do it too, often without meaning to.

The fix

Fix the questions and the rules for reading the answers first. Then report every planned result, whatever it shows.

You can tell a planned test from a lucky find.

Every file I used is free on Medicare's website. That's great for openness, but it sets a trap. If you try enough different ways of slicing data, something will look interesting just by chance. Flip a coin enough times and you'll get six heads in a row. If you then report only the six heads, you've misled people, and you may have misled yourself first. Researchers fall into this all the time without meaning to. A well-known 2011 paper by Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed how easy it is. The fix is to commit in advance. Decide the questions, the tests, and what each result will mean. Then report all of it, including results that are boring or inconvenient. That way, when I tell you something was found, you know it was a shot I called, not a ball I pointed at after it dropped.
